WordPress or a custom website?

Both options can work well, but for different needs. The useful question is which one suits you. Here we look at where each approach fits and the costs that become visible over time.

What separates the two approaches

WordPress is a ready-made content management system. A theme and plugins let you assemble a website relatively quickly. Many small business websites use this approach, and it can be enough for their needs.

A custom solution is built around your requirements. Each feature has a purpose. It takes more work at the start, but gives you control over the design, integrations and future development.

The choice should reflect what you need today and what you expect in a few years.

When WordPress fits

WordPress can be a practical choice when you need a straightforward website quickly and with a smaller starting budget.

  • A simple website — a few pages with contact details and services, without complex logic.
  • A smaller initial budget — you want to launch quickly and develop the website later.
  • Frequent content updates — you publish articles or edit pages yourself.
  • Standard features — established plugins cover your requirements.

When custom development pays off

A custom approach becomes valuable when your website needs more than an information display, or when brand and performance are priorities.

  • A distinctive brand — design and behaviour tailored to your business.
  • Specific functions — bookings, calculators or integrations that existing plugins do not cover well.
  • Speed and quality — control over loading times and the visitor experience.
  • Planned growth — room to add features without rebuilding the whole website.
  • Fewer dependencies — less reliance on third-party plugins and their maintainers.

Where the ongoing costs appear

The initial price is only part of the cost. A website also needs care after launch. WordPress often costs less to build, while maintenance and plugin subscriptions can add up. The total depends on the implementation and scope.

  • Plugin licences — premium features may require yearly subscriptions to retain updates and support.
  • Updates — the platform, theme and plugins need updates and compatibility checks.
  • Security — protection, monitoring and recovery need attention.
  • Hosting and speed — complex sites may need more powerful hosting.
  • Maintenance — someone needs to manage all these tasks.
  • Rebuilding — a website that no longer meets business needs may need substantial changes.

A custom website can reduce plugin licences and unnecessary components, but it still needs maintenance. Larger changes usually require a developer. Compare the actual proposed systems and their ongoing costs.

How to decide

  • How long should the website serve you? Consider both the launch deadline and future needs.
  • How distinctive should it be? A template may be enough, or your brand may require a custom design.
  • Will you need specific features? Check how well existing tools meet them.
  • Who will maintain it? Decide who handles content, updates and security.
  • Which costs matter most? Compare the upfront price with the total cost over several years.
  • How important is performance? Custom development offers more control, but implementation quality matters with either approach.

You do not have to choose forever. Starting with WordPress and moving to a custom solution as your business grows is a reasonable path.
